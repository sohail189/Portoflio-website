The Problem

Two different diseases, two different datasets, one goal: usable early risk screening

Liver and heart disease are both major, often-preventable health concerns where early risk awareness genuinely helps. But the clinical datasets used to model each condition are structurally very different — different features, different distributions, different quirks — so a single generic pipeline wasn't going to work for both.

The goal was to build two properly separate, well-validated classification pipelines and unify them behind one simple, approachable interface — while being careful never to present the result as an actual medical diagnosis.

My Approach

Independent pipelines, shared discipline

Each dataset went through its own full analysis and modeling cycle rather than a shared shortcut, since the two conditions have very different risk factors and data characteristics.

  • Ran exploratory data analysis on both datasets separately to understand feature distributions, missing values, and class imbalance before touching any model.
  • Cleaned and engineered features specific to each dataset, handling outliers and missing values in ways appropriate to each condition's clinical context.
  • Trained and compared several classification models per dataset — including logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost — evaluating on recall as well as accuracy, since missing a true risk case matters more than a false alarm in a screening tool.
  • Deployed the best-performing model for each condition behind a shared Streamlit interface, so a user can get a risk indication from a simple form.

For a screening tool, optimizing purely for accuracy is the wrong instinct — a model that misses genuine risk cases is far more costly than one that occasionally over-flags. Choosing models and thresholds based on recall, and being explicit that this is a screening aid rather than a diagnosis, were the two decisions that mattered most.

What I Learned

Key takeaways

In health-related modeling, the choice of evaluation metric is itself a design decision, not just a reporting detail. Prioritizing recall over raw accuracy meaningfully changed which model and threshold I picked, and reframed how I thought about "good performance" for this kind of task.

Framing and disclaimers matter as much as the model. Presenting a prediction clearly as a screening indicator — and not a diagnosis — is a responsibility that has to be designed into the interface, not left as an afterthought.
